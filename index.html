FST-344
the myostatin sponge

A natural protein variant that grabs myostatin — the muscle-growth brake — and neutralizes it. In animals, delivering its gene grows striking muscle. In people, the evidence is early and thin: small gene-therapy trials, no approved product, and a gray market selling vials of unverified powder. Here is the sponge, separated from the hype.

Quick read

FST-344 is the 344-amino-acid splice variant of follistatin, a protein that binds and neutralizes myostatin (plus related signals like activin). The logic is simple: sponge up the brake, free the muscle. In mice and monkeys, follistatin gene therapy produced dramatic muscle growth; small early human gene-therapy trials in muscle-wasting disease showed hints of benefit. But injectable FST-344 as sold online has no published human trials, is banned in sport, and exists only as an unregulated research chemical.

01The sponge

What FST-344 actually is

FST-344 is the full-length variant of follistatin, a protein whose day job is mopping up myostatin — the muscle-growth brake. Soak up the brake, and muscle is free to grow. That’s the theory; the human proof is another matter.

Follistatin wraps around myostatin like a clamp. FS-344 is the long variant that tends to stay where it’s made.

A sponge, not a signal

Follistatin doesn’t tell muscles to grow — it removes the order to stop growing by binding myostatin (and cousins activin and GDF-11) before they reach their receptors.

344 vs 315

Two main splice variants exist: FS-344 (full-length, sticks to cell surfaces) and FS-315 (shorter, circulates in blood). Online vials rarely specify which — or whether it’s either.

Research stage only

No follistatin product is approved. Human data comes from small gene-therapy trials — not from the injectable powder sold online, which has no published human evidence at all.

Plain English

If myostatin is the brake pedal, follistatin is a sponge wedged under it — it soaks up the brake signal before it reaches the pedal. Simple, elegant, and in animals, remarkably effective. In people buying vials, entirely unproven.

02The discovery

From fertility labs to muscle headlines

Nobody set out to find a muscle drug. Follistatin was a reproductive-biology protein that turned out to grip myostatin — and gene therapy turned that grip into remarkable animal results.

From reproductive biology to the muscle-growth spotlight.
Found far from muscle

Follistatin was discovered studying reproductive hormones — it binds activin, a fertility signal. The myostatin connection came later: same sponge, different spill.

The gene-therapy shortcut

Instead of injecting the protein, researchers delivered the follistatin gene (via AAV vectors) so muscles made their own supply. In mice and monkeys: dramatic, lasting muscle growth.

Tiny steps in people

Small early-phase trials tested AAV-follistatin in Becker muscular dystrophy and inclusion body myositis — hints of benefit, small numbers, early days. Injectable FST-344 never got even that far.

Plain English

Follistatin was hired for fertility research and moonlighted as a muscle story. The gene-therapy version — muscles brewing their own sponge — is the only form with any human data, and it’s a far cry from a vial of powder bought online.

03How it’s supposed to work

The sponge effect

Clamp myostatin, block the brake, grow muscle. The chain is short and logical — and proven only where the protein is made on-site (gene therapy in animals), not where it’s injected from a vial.

The interception — follistatin works OUTSIDE the cell

Myostatin/activin → ActRIIB receptor → SMAD2/3 → nucleus. FST344 grabs the ligands outside the cell — the receptor never fires, so it never enters the cell or touches genes directly.
The sponge mechanism — clean in animals; the injectable version unproven in people.

Myostatin vs Activin — one sponge, several spills

Follistatin is routinely sold as a "myostatin inhibitor." That's true but incomplete: it also grabs activin — a signal with jobs in reproduction and development. Broader binding means broader unknowns, and it's why muscle-growth claims built on "just blocking myostatin" oversimplify the biology.
Follistatin grabs both — which is why “myostatin inhibitor” undersells it, and why the unknowns reach beyond muscle.

Isoforms — same family, different travel plans

Same protein family, different behavior: 344 anchors locally, 315 travels. A vial labeled “follistatin” rarely says which.
Not picky

Follistatin also sponges up activin and GDF-11 — signals with jobs in reproduction and development. A broad sponge mops up more than the intended spill.

Local vs circulating

FS-344 stays near where it’s made (it grips cell surfaces); FS-315 travels in blood. Which variant — and which behavior — an online vial even contains is anyone’s guess.

The delivery problem

Proteins injected under the skin face enzymes, clearance, and distribution hurdles. Gene therapy dodges this by making the protein on-site — a trick a vial can’t copy.

Plain English

The mechanism is beautifully simple: clamp the brake signal, free the muscle. But follistatin is a broad sponge in a body full of spills — and getting the sponge to the right place, intact, is the part the vial story skips.

Gene therapy: real signals

The AAV-follistatin approach has genuine animal results and early human hints. It’s also a one-time gene treatment — not a vial.

Injectable FST-344: zero

No published human trial of the injectable product sold online. The animal gene-therapy halo does not transfer to a powder.

The variant confusion

Trials used gene-delivered FS-344/315; vials claim “FST-344” with no verification. Same name, different universe of evidence.

Plain English

Imagine a brilliant stage play (gene therapy) and a fan selling photocopied scripts outside (vials). The play got good reviews; the photocopy has never been performed. Fans keep quoting the reviews as if they apply to the copy.

✅ What it does

  • Binds myostatin, activin, and GDF-11 in lab and animal studies — the core chemistry is genuinely established.
  • Grows striking muscle in animals when delivered by gene therapy (AAV) — the strongest evidence in the field.
  • Stays local as FS-344, gripping cell surfaces near where it’s produced.

🚫 What it doesn’t do

  • Enter muscle cells and switch genes on — it works outside the cell, on ligands, never directly on genes.
  • Have any published human trial as an injectable — zero. The vial evidence file is empty.
  • Only block myostatin — it also sponges activin, a signal with reproductive and developmental roles.
05Potential benefits

Established science

What is firmly established is chemistry: follistatin binds myostatin, activin, and GDF-11 in the lab. A binding fact is a reason to run experiments, not a benefit.

Muscle-growth potential

Predicted / emerging

Predicted / emerging

Multi-signal interception

Think of it this way: myostatin is a speed-limit sign your muscles obey. Follistatin throws a towel over the sign — predicted to let muscles drive faster, but the only vehicles tested were gene-therapy animals and a handful of trial patients.

06Who it may suit

FST-344 has no established indication — it is purely investigational, and no human trial of the injectable product has ever been published. Two tiles: who the research actually involves, and where extra caution is warranted. This is general information, not personal medical advice.

08Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

A sponge that grabs several signals at once, sold without oversight, with no human safety data for the injectable form. Here’s the risk picture in three boxes.

Observed

Banned in sport

Observed fact: WADA prohibits follistatin and myostatin inhibitors. Tested athletes have no gray area — the ban is explicit.

Observed

Unregulated vials

Observed fact: injectable “FST-214/344” has no quality oversight. Independent testing of gray-market peptides repeatedly finds wrong doses, impurities, or the wrong compound.

Theoretical

A broad sponge

Theoretical: follistatin also neutralizes activin, which regulates reproduction and other systems. Mopping up activin could have reproductive and developmental effects nobody has studied.

Theoretical

Growth without brakes

Theoretical: sustained growth-signal release is the textbook setting for abnormal cell growth concerns. Unproven for follistatin — but the mechanism invites the question.

Unknown

Long-term effects

Unknown: no chronic-use data in humans for follistatin of any kind. Months or years of sponging multiple signals is uncharted.

Unknown

Immunogenicity

Unknown: injecting a foreign protein repeatedly can provoke immune reactions or neutralizing antibodies — studied for approved biologics, never for gray-market follistatin.

Plain English

Known: banned in sport, unregulated vials. Theoretical: a broad sponge in a body full of signals it also mops up. Unknown: almost everything else — including what the immune system thinks of repeated mystery-protein injections.

10Terminology

Understanding FST-344 Terminology

Twelve terms, each with the scientific definition and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceFollistatin is a naturally occurring protein that binds and neutralizes several TGF-beta-family ligands, including myostatin, activins, and GDF-11. It was originally identified in ovarian follicular fluid for its role in suppressing follicle-stimulating hormone, long before its connection to muscle was known. Follistatin works extracellularly, intercepting these signals before they can reach their receptors. Because it neutralizes multiple ligands at once, its effects are broad and inherently less selective than targeting myostatin alone.

Plain EnglishFollistatin is a protein your body makes that acts like a sponge for several growth-restraint signals at once — myostatin, activins, and GDF-11. Scientists first found it in the reproductive system, years before anyone linked it to muscles. It works outside the cells, soaking up signals before they can deliver their messages. Because it grabs several signals instead of just one, its effects are broader and harder to predict.

The scienceFST-344 is the full-length, 344-amino-acid splice variant of follistatin. Compared with the shorter FS-315 variant, it binds cell-surface structures and tends to anchor locally at the site where it is produced or delivered. Human research on follistatin has involved gene-therapy delivery of FS-344 or FS-315 in small early-phase trials for muscle conditions. No FST-344 protein product has been approved, and unregulated products sold under the name have no verified identity or quality.

Plain EnglishFST-344 is the full-length, 344-amino-acid version of follistatin. The long version tends to stick to cell surfaces and act locally, while the shorter FS-315 circulates more freely. The only human research involved gene therapy delivering these variants in small early trials — not vials of protein. Nothing called FST-344 is an approved product, and unregulated versions have no verified content.

The scienceMyostatin, formally GDF-8, is a TGF-beta-family signaling protein and the principal negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass. It signals through ActRIIB and SMAD2/3 to restrain muscle growth. Follistatin binds myostatin extracellularly and prevents it from reaching its receptors, which is the basis of the muscle-interest story. But follistatin also neutralizes activins and other relatives, so its effects cannot be reduced to myostatin blockade alone.

Plain EnglishMyostatin is the muscle-growth brake — the signal that tells muscles to stop growing. Follistatin soaks it up before it can reach muscle cells, which is why follistatin got famous in muscle circles. But follistatin grabs several other signals too, not just myostatin. So calling follistatin a myostatin blocker leaves out half the story.

The scienceActivins are TGF-beta-family signaling proteins that regulate reproduction, embryonic development, inflammation, and tissue repair. They signal through the same activin receptors used by myostatin. Follistatin binds activins with high affinity and is one of their principal natural regulators. Any intervention using follistatin therefore affects activin-dependent systems — including fertility and hormonal balance — not just muscle.

Plain EnglishActivins are chemical messengers involved in reproduction, development, and healing. They use the same receptors as myostatin, and follistatin binds them tightly — which is why follistatin cannot be treated as a muscle-only tool. Using follistatin inevitably touches activin-driven systems like fertility and hormones. That is the collateral that muscle-focused marketing leaves out.

The scienceA splice variant is one of several protein versions produced from a single gene by alternative RNA splicing. During gene expression, a gene's coding segments can be combined in different ways, yielding related but distinct proteins. FS-344 and FS-315 are alternative splice variants of the follistatin gene, differing in length and in how they distribute through tissue. The existence of multiple variants means a label reading only follistatin specifies little about what is actually present.

Plain EnglishA splice variant is one of several protein versions made from the same gene, assembled from different combinations of its parts. Follistatin comes in at least two: the long FS-344 and the short FS-315, which behave differently in the body. That matters because a label saying just follistatin does not tell you which version you are looking at. And with unregulated products, it may tell you nothing at all.

The scienceAn AAV vector is an adeno-associated virus engineered to carry genetic instructions into cells without causing disease. AAVs are favored gene-delivery vehicles because they rarely integrate into host DNA and provoke relatively mild immune responses. In follistatin research, AAV vectors delivered the FS-344 or FS-315 gene into muscle tissue so the cells would produce follistatin themselves. This approach is fundamentally different from injecting the protein directly.

Plain EnglishAn AAV vector is a harmless virus reprogrammed to ferry new genetic instructions into cells. Researchers used it to deliver the follistatin gene into muscle tissue, so the muscles made their own follistatin. That is gene therapy — a completely different idea from injecting follistatin protein from a vial. The human follistatin trials that actually happened used this viral-delivery approach.

The scienceGene therapy is a treatment strategy that delivers genetic instructions into a patient's cells so the body produces a therapeutic protein itself. It is distinct from conventional drug delivery, where the protein or chemical is manufactured and then injected. Follistatin gene therapy for muscle-wasting conditions used AAV vectors to introduce follistatin variants into muscle in small early-phase human trials. These remain investigational: no follistatin gene therapy has been approved.

Plain EnglishGene therapy means giving cells new instructions so they make the medicine themselves, instead of injecting the medicine directly. For follistatin, this meant using a virus to deliver the follistatin gene into muscles in small early human trials. It is an entirely different approach from buying a vial of protein. And like everything else here, it remains experimental — no follistatin gene therapy is approved.

The scienceA ligand trap is an agent that captures a signaling molecule before it can reach its receptor, blocking the signal without touching the receptor itself. Follistatin is a naturally occurring ligand trap for myostatin, activins, and GDF-11, intercepting them in the extracellular space. Engineered ligand traps — such as soluble ActRIIB decoys — copy this same strategy as drugs. Follistatin's trap action is inherently broad, since it neutralizes several ligands at once.

Plain EnglishA ligand trap is anything that intercepts a chemical message before it reaches its destination — nature's version of a spam filter. Follistatin is the body's own ligand trap for myostatin and several of its relatives. Drug companies built synthetic versions of the same idea, like free-floating copies of the ActRIIB receptor. Follistatin's version is powerful but indiscriminate: it grabs several messages, not just one.

The scienceThe World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is the international body that sets and enforces rules against performance-enhancing drugs in sport. Myostatin inhibitors, including follistatin and agents that increase follistatin expression, are prohibited at all times under WADA's S4 category for hormone and metabolic modulators. The ban covers the strategy of myostatin blockade generally, not just specific products. Tested athletes using follistatin face sanctions regardless of the product's regulatory status.

Plain EnglishWADA is the global anti-doping authority that decides what is banned in sport. It prohibits myostatin inhibitors — including follistatin — at all times, under its S4 category for hormone and metabolic modulators. The ban targets the whole strategy of blocking myostatin, not just particular products. For tested athletes, that is a hard no, full stop.

The scienceThe term research chemical is used on the gray market for substances sold with a for-research-use-only label that implies no human use. These products have no approved medical indication, no regulated manufacturing standards, and no verified identity or purity. FST-344 sold through such channels exists in this category. The label provides no meaningful safety information and does not make the substance legal or safe for personal use.

Plain EnglishOn the gray market, research chemical is a legal fig leaf printed on substances sold with no approved use and no quality oversight. Nobody verifies what is actually in the vial, and the label tells you nothing about safety. FST-344 sold this way sits squarely in that category. For research only does not make it safe — it just makes it unaccountable.

The sciencePreclinical research refers to the stages of investigation conducted before any human testing: experiments in cultured cells and in animal models. Most of follistatin's muscle-related evidence comes from this stage, including studies in mice where follistatin increased muscle mass. Preclinical results establish biological plausibility but do not predict human efficacy or safety. The history of muscle research is full of findings that looked promising in animals and failed in people.

Plain EnglishPreclinical means the research that happens before any human is involved — cells in dishes and animals in labs. Almost everything known about follistatin and muscle comes from this stage, including mouse studies showing bigger muscles. Preclinical work can prove an idea is biologically plausible, but it cannot prove it works in people. Muscle science is littered with animal successes that flopped in humans.

The scienceImmunogenicity is the capacity of a substance — particularly an injected protein — to provoke an immune response. The immune system may generate antibodies against a foreign or repeatedly administered protein, reducing its effect or causing reactions. This is a standard, closely monitored concern for all biologic drugs. For gray-market follistatin products, immunogenicity has not been studied, so any immune consequences are entirely unknown.

Plain EnglishImmunogenicity is the risk that your immune system recognizes an injected protein as foreign and attacks it. That can neutralize the protein, or worse, trigger allergic-style reactions. Every legitimate biologic drug is tested for this carefully. Gray-market follistatin has never been tested at all — so what the immune system does about it is a complete unknown.
